# Herbivore

***

Keiji’s alarm went off at seven.

He lay on his futon and watched the ceiling. The room was narrow. A thin winter light slipped through the curtains.

His parents slept in western beds in the next room. They said it was easier on their knees. Keiji’s futon had to be folded each morning and set out to air. He pictured himself lifting it, slapping it, draping it over the balcony rail. The thought made him press the snooze button.

The alarm stopped. The quiet returned. He fell back into sleep without dreams.

When it rang again, he got up.

The bathroom was cold. He turned on the heater and waited for the air to change. Then he slid open the door to the tub room.

In many Japanese homes the bath is not a single place. You wash first, sitting on a small stool, with a hand shower. Only after you are clean do you enter the deep soaking tub. The hot water is shared by the household. It is meant for quiet, not soap.

Keiji sat and rinsed, slow and careful. Then he lowered himself into the water.

It rose to his chest. The heat moved into his shoulders and neck. He breathed out and watched steam bead on the tiles.

His phone buzzed on the shelf by the mirror.

A reminder: an online lecture in fifteen minutes.

The professor was in California. Berkeley. The class ran on Zoom. It had been hard to get a spot. Keiji’s adviser had written an email for him, the kind of small favor that could decide a schedule.

Keiji read the reminder. He set the phone face down.

He stayed in the tub.

Time passed in small pieces. He watched ripples settle. He listened to water touch the acrylic wall.

As a child he used to stay until his fingers turned “wrinkly”. He held his hands up and stared at the skin, proud of the proof that he had lasted longer than anyone else. His mother would knock and tell him to hurry. His father would laugh and say, let him enjoy it.

His fingers wrinkled now, too. It was not a victory. It was just time.

When he finally got out, the air felt sharp. He dried off, dressed, and padded back to his room.

The lecture had started. The small blue notification sat in the corner of his screen like a polite accusation. He did not open it.

Instead he picked up PopEye from the low table and flipped through. He liked the clean photos and the lists of things he did not need.

A page caught his eye: a Korean product called Dare2BSmooth, designed for skin irritation after waxing. The copy showed a smooth torso and a small tube held like a secret.

Keiji touched his chest through his shirt. The skin had been red for days. He had thought it would fade by itself.

He read the name again. Dare2BSmooth.

That became the day.

He dressed for the street, put his phone and wallet in his pockets, and left.

Outside, his neighborhood was still half asleep. A narrow road ran between low buildings with balconies and laundry poles. A vending machine hummed beside a closed barber shop. Bicycles leaned against a wall, their baskets empty. Utility wires crossed the sky in black loops.

He walked past a small shrine tucked between apartment blocks. Someone had left a can of coffee as an offering. A stray cat watched him from under a parked car and did not move.

At the station entrance he went down into the warm smell of concrete and metal. Signs pointed in neat English and Japanese. He tapped his transit card and joined the flow.

The Ginza Line platform was already crowded. People stood in lines marked on the floor. When the train arrived, it did not look like it could take more. It did anyway.

Keiji was carried in with the rest. Coats pressed against coats. A scarf brushed his cheek. He found a strap and held it with two fingers, careful not to touch anyone more than he had to.

At Ueno he slipped out, followed arrows, and changed to the Yamanote Line. The green loop line ran like a belt around the city. The platform was louder, filled with announcements and footsteps.

The next train came. This one was crowded too, but in a different way. The passengers looked like they had already decided what their day was for.

Keiji watched station names flash past. The city slid by in fragments—convenience stores, office towers, a river, the edge of a park.

When he reached Shibuya, he climbed up into daylight with the others.

The station opened into a bright rush. Screens played ads on every side. Music leaked from storefronts. People moved fast, as if all of them had been given the same deadline.

He followed the signs and found the crossing.

Shibuya Scramble was a square of white stripes and people. When the lights changed, the crowd poured in from every direction. Tourists held up phones. A couple posed in the middle and laughed. Someone in a suit stepped around them without looking.

Keiji crossed with the flow.

He noticed the foreigners first. Their faces stood out. Some moved like they were trying to take in everything at once, as if the city would disappear if they did not capture it.

Keiji wondered what they had come for. He felt a small irritation, thin as paper.

He was a little shorter than the men around him. In the crossing he mostly saw backs and shoulders. The world was a wall of fabric and moving arms. Above them, a screen showed a smiling idol holding a drink.

He made it to the other side and kept walking.

He found the store after checking his phone twice. The clerk pointed him to a shelf of skincare. The tube was there, tucked between brands with soft names.

Dare2BSmooth.

He held it, then looked at the price.

It was more than he expected. He stood for a moment as if the number might change. Then he took it to the counter, paid, and left with the small bag in his hand.

On the way back he stopped at a 7-Eleven. The automatic door chimed. Warm air hit his face. The smell of fried food and coffee was familiar and dull.

He went to the aisle with pens and picked out Bic Terrapen pens. Seven of them, one by one.

At the counter he asked, in a quiet voice, if they had more.

The worker blinked, then disappeared into the back. Keiji waited by the register, hands resting on the counter edge, eyes on the candy rack.

The worker returned with a sealed pack of forty.

“All of them?” the worker asked, half amused.

Keiji nodded. With the seven from the shelf, it made forty-seven.

The worker rang them up. The total flashed on the screen. Keiji paid without comment and left with a bag heavy enough to swing.

Outside, the city looked the same as before, as if nothing had happened.

***

Keiji studied Civil Engineering at the University of Tokyo.

The lecture hall was old but clean. Desks folded down in front of each seat. The air carried a mix of plastic, dry paper, and winter coats.

That day’s lecture was in materials science.

On the screen, the professor drew a curve. Stress on one axis, strain on the other. He spoke about the point where material stopped behaving politely. He wrote a simple formula and said the name of a law.

Keiji copied the symbols without reading them.

He sat near the back. From there he could see most of the room, but he did not look at most of it.

Aiko sat diagonally forward and to his left.

She wrote in a small notebook. When she shifted in her seat, her skirt moved with her. It was an “unnecessarily short skirt”, the kind the school did not officially allow but never stopped. Her legs looked light and narrow, as if they belonged to a different season.

Keiji watched the corner of her page. He watched her pen move. He watched the way she tucked hair behind her ear without looking up.

The professor said something about elasticity. Keiji heard the words as sound.

When the break came, students stood and stretched. Chairs scraped. Voices rose.

Min-soo came to Keiji’s row and leaned against the desk.

Min-soo’s Japanese was fluent, but some vowels came out soft. It was small, but it was there. Keiji heard it.

Min-soo glanced toward Aiko and then back.

“You’re doing it again,” he said.

“Doing what.”

“Looking.”

Keiji shrugged. “I’m looking at the screen.”

Min-soo snorted. “Sure.”

He lowered his voice. “If you want to talk to her, just do it. Be direct. People like that.”

Keiji did not answer. He stared at his notes, lines of symbols that meant nothing.

Min-soo waited, then added, “Or… I could. Maybe.”

Keiji finally looked at him. “Then just talk to her. You sit near her in seminar.”

Min-soo’s mouth tightened. “It’s not that simple.”

“Why not.”

Min-soo hesitated. He lifted one shoulder, like he was talking about the weather.

“You know how I sound,” he said.

“Your accent? It’s barely there.”

“Barely is still there.”

Keiji watched him.

“So,” Keiji said.

Min-soo looked down at his hands. “People notice. They always do. Eventually.”

“That doesn’t mean she’d care.”

“Maybe.”

After a beat he added, quieter, “Or maybe she’d care later. That’s usually when it happens.”

Keiji did not argue.

Aiko laughed at something her friend said. The sound rose and fell and was gone.

Keiji spoke as if the story was not his.

“A couple weeks ago she dropped her pencil,” he said. “I picked it up.”

Min-soo nodded.

“She said thank you,” Keiji continued. “Then she asked if I was in this class.”

Min-soo’s expression did not change. It did not need to.

“She didn’t know my name,” Keiji said.

Min-soo tapped the desk with two fingers.

“Yeah,” he said. “I get it.”

The break ended. People sat down. The professor returned to the front. The curve on the screen waited like a warning.

***

That evening Keiji sat in the kitchen with his mother.

The kitchen was small and bright. A kettle hissed on the stove. A low table held a bowl of tangerines and a stack of mail.

Yuki watched television. She wore a cardigan and reading glasses. Every so often she reached for her tea cup without looking, as if her hand already knew where it was.

Keiji had his laptop open. One eye on the screen, one on the TV. He clicked through channels in a slow loop, not staying long enough to become interested.

A host appeared on a set lit like a jewelry store. He had a smooth voice and a sharp suit. The show looked like an American talk show, only compressed into Japanese politeness and bright variety.

The host smiled at the camera.

“Tonight,” he said, “we’re talking about “Herbivore men”.”

The words sat on the screen in neat text. The studio audience made a sound that was half laugh, half sigh.

Yuki glanced at Keiji. “That again,” she said, not unkindly.

Keiji stopped clicking.

The host turned to a guest seated on a couch. The guest was in his thirties, in plain clothes, with an easy smile. He looked like someone who had chosen his own pace and did not apologize for it.

“So,” the host said, “people call you a sōshoku danshi. A herbivore. Why?”

The guest leaned forward, elbows on his knees. “First, it’s a funny label,” he said. “Like we’re in a zoo.”

Laughter.

“And second,” he went on, “it started as an observation. In the mid-2000s, a writer noticed that some young men weren’t chasing the old script. Not chasing promotions. Not chasing dates. Not chasing the idea that a man has to conquer something to be a man.”

The host lifted his brows. “So you don’t want romance.”

“It’s not that,” the guest said. “Some do. Some don’t. The point is we don’t treat it like a test. We don’t treat women like prizes. We don’t treat ourselves like machines.”

A few claps from the audience.

The host tilted his head. “But Japan needs babies. Japan needs energy. Japan needs—”

“Japan needs a lot of things,” the guest said.

More laughter.

He continued, “People say we’re passive. That we’re weak. That we’re lazy. But a lot of us work. A lot of us care about art, friends, hobbies, family. We just don’t want to spend our whole lives proving ourselves in the same way.”

The host leaned in. “And what do you say to critics who think herbivores are harmless?”

The guest looked straight into the camera for a second, as if enjoying the moment. Then he said, “herbivores can bite too”.

The audience applauded. Even the host laughed, surprised by the bluntness.

Yuki’s hand paused on her tea cup. Keiji watched without moving.

The host was about to speak again when the front door slid open.

Takeshi came in early.

He always came in late, shoulders heavy, tie loosened. Tonight he came in as if he had forgotten the shape of the day.

He set his bag down slowly. He did not take off his coat.

Yuki stood. “You’re home early,” she said.

Takeshi nodded once.

“They fired me,” he said.

The TV kept talking in the background. Someone in the studio laughed at a joke that no longer belonged to the room.


***

For a moment nobody spoke.

Yuki’s face stayed still, as if she were waiting for a second sentence that would explain the first.

Keiji closed his laptop without thinking. The click of the lid sounded too loud.

“Epson?” Yuki asked, as if there might be another company.

Takeshi nodded. “Downsizing,” he said. He said it like a foreign word.

“But the printer,” Keiji said. “The new one. The one with the AI—”

Takeshi’s eyes shifted to him. They were tired, but clear.

“They’ll finish it without me,” he said. “Or they won’t. It’s not my problem now.”

Yuki pulled out a chair and sat, careful and straight. “What happens next.”

Takeshi exhaled. “Severance,” he said. “It’s… generous. I keep insurance for a while. Enough to cover my heart meds.”

Keiji had known about the heart condition. It had been a fact that hovered in the house like an appliance. Something always on, never discussed.

“So much for lifetime employment,” Yuki said.

Takeshi did not argue. He looked at the tangerines and did not touch them.

Keiji felt something tighten behind his ribs. It did not become words.

He stood and left the kitchen. No one stopped him.

In his room he shut the door and turned the lock. Then he sat on the floor, back against the wall, knees up.

His eyes filled without sound. The tears came and stayed there, held back by pride or habit. He wiped them with the sleeve of his shirt.

He thought of his father carrying him.

He was six again, in Hokkaido.

They had rented a small wooden hut near the hills. His mother had stayed behind that day, warm in the room with a book and tea. Keiji and his father had gone out.

The air had been clean and cold. Snow lay in patches in the shade. Their breath came out white.

Takeshi walked ahead, sure-footed. When Keiji slowed, Takeshi lifted him up and set him on his shoulders.

Keiji had laughed, high above the world. He held his father’s head like a handle.

They followed a trail through low trees and then out to open land. Wind came off the sea.

At the coast the water stretched flat and gray. Waves broke and left foam like torn paper.

Keiji pointed. “What’s there?”

Takeshi squinted into the distance as if he could see through weather.

“Over there,” he said, “if you go far enough, you reach Russia.”

“Russia?” Keiji repeated.

Takeshi nodded. “A long way,” he said. “But it’s there.”

Keiji thought for a moment, then asked, “Did we fight them?”

Takeshi smiled at the logic. “A long time ago,” he said. “There was a battle at sea. Tsushima. Japan won.”

Keiji tried the word. “Tsu-shi-ma.”

“That’s right,” Takeshi said. He adjusted Keiji’s legs on his shoulders.

Keiji looked at the water again. “Will Japan be great again?”

Takeshi laughed softly, not at him, but at the weight of the question in a child’s mouth.

“Great,” he said, “is a hard word.”

Keiji waited.

“I think it’s more important to be happy,” Takeshi said. “Being great doesn’t keep you warm.”

Keiji did not understand, but he listened.

Takeshi turned his head a little. “If I get to see you grow up,” he said, “and maybe one day meet your family… maybe grandkids.”

He made a small face, a wink, exaggerated for a child.

Keiji giggled, embarrassed without knowing why.

“That,” Takeshi said, “would make me very happy.”

Keiji blinked in his room and the coast was gone.

He looked at the locked door. He listened to the house. There were no footsteps. Only the low hum of the refrigerator through the wall.

***

Keiji waited until the apartment was quiet.

Then he took the bag from the corner and set it on the floor.

Inside were the pens.

He opened one and took it apart with calm hands. He pressed the ink cartridge over a small jar and squeezed. Thick ink pooled like tar.

He repeated the motion again and again. The work was slow. It required attention. He liked that.

When the jar was partly full, he capped it and cleaned the table. He wiped away every dark smear.

He sat down at his computer.

He did not do anything dramatic. He moved in small careful steps, as if trying not to wake someone. He avoided tying what he did to his name.

He found a listing on a site written in Chinese. The product name was long and technical: Cyclopropylidene oxynitrate. The page showed numbers and shipping options and a warning paragraph he did not fully read.

He placed the order.

When it was done, he cleared what he could and shut down his computer.

He lay back on his futon and stared at the ceiling again.

A hut in Hokkaido drifted into his mind. A small place by a meadow. No train announcements. No screens. Just wind and the sound of snow under boots.

Surely there was unclaimed land somewhere. Land no one cared about. Land where a person could build a simple room and keep warm.

He pictured an Akita dog in the doorway, thick fur, steady eyes. The dog would wait for him. The dog would not ask questions.

He fell asleep thinking that this was a plan.


***

Another day.

Keiji met Min-soo after class. They walked to a bookshop that sold manga in neat rows. They stood close to the shelves without speaking much, reading the same pages twice.

Their favorite series had a new installment coming out next week for everyone. But the author was giving a talk in Tokyo that afternoon.

They decided to go.

They entered the Marunouchi Line at a station with clean white tiles. The platform was calm compared to the lines Keiji usually took.

The train arrived with space inside.

They sat across from each other. An old man dozed with his mouth slightly open.

Keiji held a hanging ring with two fingers as the train started. The ring swung a little. He watched his hand.

His pinky nail was not evenly cut. One edge was jagged, catching on the fabric of his sleeve.

He stared at it, then looked away.

Next time, he decided, he would use a nail file instead of just clipping.

Min-soo talked about the author’s last interview. Keiji nodded at the right times.

They got off and walked through an office district where winter sunlight reflected off glass. They joined a small line of fans outside a modest hall.

Inside, the author spoke about deadlines, assistants, and the way a character can surprise you. People laughed. People clapped. Keiji listened and, for a short while, felt like a normal person.

They returned home after dark.

Min-soo peeled off at his station with a small wave. Keiji watched him go, then took the stairs to his apartment.

That night he locked his door.

The desk lamp made a circle of light. He opened his laptop. The screen filled his face with blue.

He had gathered pieces over weeks. Public documents. Old manuals. Code fragments left where they should not have been. He did not think of it as stealing. He thought of it as studying.

He ran Claude Code, an AI coding assistant, alongside his searches. It suggested patterns. It highlighted things that looked weak. It was patient and literal, and it sometimes failed.

Keiji had to prod it. He had to ask the same question in different ways. He had to remove the polite words and make the prompt sharp.

Eventually it pointed to a gap.

The system still demanded a multi factor authentication before it would accept anything important.

Keiji stared at the note on the screen and felt a small satisfaction that did not reach his face.

He closed the window and leaned back.

He already had the second factor.

He had gotten it from Min-soo.

It had started months ago, with a casual conversation after class.

Keiji had mentioned he was thinking about a career in transit systems. It sounded like ambition. It sounded respectable.

Min-soo perked up. His father worked for the metro authority. He complained about long hours. He complained about old systems that never died. He complained about warnings that no one listened to.

Keiji listened as if he cared about Min-soo’s life.

A week later, Keiji asked for help settling a debate. Nothing serious. Just whether newer IC cards had better protection than the older admin keycards. Academic curiosity. A question for someone with insider knowledge.

Min-soo hesitated.

But they were friends. Keiji had never asked him for anything before.

Min-soo brought the card the next day, wrapped in a handkerchief, like it might pick up fingerprints from the air.

Keiji returned it that night.

Three hours was all he needed.

***

It was the last day of the year.

Tokyo felt busy and tired at the same time. Stations were full of people holding small gifts. A few wore kimono under their coats, sleeves tucked so they would not brush the floor.

Keiji rode the Ginza Line.

He stood near the door with a bag at his feet. His phone was in his hand, screen dimmed.

A small message appeared.

A simple signal. A regular check-in.

A “heartbeat”.

He watched it arrive and disappear.

He thought of the metro’s security. How many layers of procedure existed mostly on paper. How many decisions had been made by people who would retire before the consequences arrived.

Why couldn’t they hire decent programmers, he thought.

The thought did not make him loud. It made him calm.

The train rocked through a curve. A woman steadied herself with a hand on the pole. Someone’s shopping bag brushed Keiji’s shin.

His mind drifted to a story his mother had once explained carefully, in the way adults explain frightening things to children.

In 1995, a cult released poison on the Tokyo subway. Morning commuters collapsed. People stumbled out of stations with eyes watering, unable to see. The city learned, for a while, that it was not invulnerable.

Keiji had read more later. He had watched old news footage. He remembered the masks, the stretchers, the reporters speaking with tight voices.

They were amateurs, he thought. They had wanted a spectacle. They had believed in something.

The train slowed as it approached Shimbashi.

Keiji’s thumb moved.

He pressed a button on his phone.

In his bag, something activated. Heat, then smoke.

At first no one noticed.

The smoke was thick and white. It seeped out low, not rising like steam but spreading along the floor, heavy as fog.

Keiji watched it and noted how it stayed near the ground. He noted how long it took for a passenger to look down.

He was proud that his calculations were correct.

Then someone coughed.

A man near the far door glanced at the floor and frowned. He lifted his foot, as if he had stepped into something wet.

A woman’s voice rose. “What is that.”

People shifted away from the smoke. Shoes scraped. A stroller wheel rolled through it and vanished.

Keiji did not move.

***

He remembered Hokkaido again.

Not the coast this time.

He and his father had walked through a shallow valley. The land opened into a meadow. Low fog lay over the grass and moved in soft sheets as wind passed.

It looked like a sea made of cloud.

Keiji could see his hands in front of him. He could not see his feet.

Takeshi had laughed, delighted by something so simple. He waved his arm through the fog, carving a tunnel that closed behind his hand.

For a moment the world was small. The air smelled like wet earth and pine.

Keiji realized, even at six, that his father was happy.

He realized now, at twenty-four, that it might have been the happiest day of their lives. For both of them.

The memory faded as a shout pulled him back.

The smoke was waist-deep.

People screamed.

Someone slammed a hand against the emergency intercom. Another person tried the door lever. A man pushed forward and recoiled when the smoke touched his face.

Keiji put on his Sony headphones. The seal pressed against his ears, cutting the noise down to a dull roar.

Still, he could hear them.

He thought, flatly, “so much for the best in the world noise cancellation”.

The train did not stop.

Normally there were systems that would cut power, brake hard, open doors. Normally there were rules and redundancies and alarms.

Keiji watched the tunnel lights flick past at even intervals.

The train kept gathering speed, as if it had been told to ignore fear.

The smell reached him then. Sharp and ugly. Like rotten eggs left too long in a closed room.

His throat tightened. His eyes watered.

A woman fell to her knees and crawled, groping for air above the fog. A man lifted his coat over his face and stumbled toward the door, hitting the pole, bouncing off it, trying again.

Keiji stayed where he was.

In his mind, the talk-show guest’s smile returned. The bright set. The applause.

“herbivores can bite too”.

The rocking of the car changed, as if the train were leaning into something that had no space for it.

Keiji tasted metal.

Hands pounded the doors. Someone tried to climb onto a seat.

The smoke rose in slow layers, swallowing knees, then hips, then chests.

The tunnel ahead brightened. The suggestion of a platform appeared around a bend. Beyond it, another set of lights did not match the wall.

Keiji’s breaths came shorter.

The rotten smell filled the space between them. The fog pressed against his face. His lungs fought and lost ground.

The car surged forward, steady and blind, toward the place where rails met and choices disappeared.
