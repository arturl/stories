# The Dogs Of Kolkata

## Act One — The Dogs' Hour

Sutanuti, Calcutta. April 1946.

In the early morning, the lanes of Chitpur Road belong to the desi dogs. They come out early, 
before chai wallahs, before the first electric trams start. 

They know the neighborhood and move without hurry, not wasting time or energy. They remember which doorstep, which 
courtyard yielded something before, and might again.

A dog sniffs the sidewalk where a street merchant sold biryani yesterday, then moves on. Another sits at the steps of
the police station, waiting for something that has a smell if not the shape. The constable 
will not arrive for another two hours.

The dogs are of different sizes and colors but look the same: slim build, pie-dish ears and a curved tail.
The street has produced a breed optimized for survival. 

On the wall near the police station, a Muslim League poster. The paper is still wet. The dogs pass
it without interest.

The streets come to life in a few hours: kathi rolls, jhal muri and chai. Until then, only the smell of the river Hooghly, a mile west, reaches here. The air is stifling. The monsoon will not arrive for another two months.

A girl comes out of the courtyard with a rice bowl. Slender, pale skin. A white tunic, a scarf over her shoulders, sandals a bit too big for her feet.
She leans forward and places the bowl on the ground. "Sharing your breakfast with the dogs again, Farah?" An older woman puts her hand on the girl's shoulder. "I'm not hungry, mother." "What kind of food is this for a dog, really? Let me see what else we have." 
The woman goes inside the house and comes back with a plate. Chicken feet. 

A young dog, tanned brown, comes closer. The woman stretches her hands out, inviting it to come closer, but the 
dog keeps its distance. "Come!" says Farah, and the dog wags its tail, comes closer and starts eating. First, the chicken feet. The dog digs into the food, tossing its head up to swallow the pieces. It doesn't savor, just consumes. "This dog 
likes you," says the woman. "It's the smallest of the pack, mom. Look at the scar on his thigh - this is another dog's bite."
The dog eats fast. The chicken feet are gone, then the rice bowl is licked clean. It steps back a few feet, lingers. Will 
there be more? "Time for school, Farah." The woman wraps her hand around the girl's shoulder. Both of them head back inside.

Farah exits the house carrying a gray cloth bag. She crosses the street and heads toward the school. The day starts with the Quran class. The teacher is an old scholar, thick glasses, hard on hearing. He might not even notice if she is late for class. 

She thinks about Nikhil, their evening together yesterday. She remembers the small droplets of sweat on his neck. Always confident, a "man of a few words" as he calls himself jokingly, what happened to him? Silly words coming out of his mouth, voice trembling. For the first time, she felt like the grownup of the two.

They've been on that rooftop before. Nikhil's parents use it for drying clothes. A couple of stools, a bench. A good place to take in the city - the local minaret, the Hooghly if you stand on your tiptoes - all without being seen.

"Have you even been with a woman?" she asked. Why did she ask that, to hurt him? Not a fully grown-up woman herself. It's true what her mother said, girls do grow up earlier. Only six months older than Nikhil, the age difference suddenly felt much bigger. Of course he hasn't. His bravado gone, blushing. "Why does it matter?" 

Her own question surprised her. It was harsh. Perhaps it broke something between them, or made it stronger.

She hears the school bell and hurries. The watchman is waving her in.
