# Chicago River - Plot

## Genre
Noir thriller / literary fiction

---

## Characters

### Omar Al-Rashidi
- 27 years old
- Born in Baghdad, emigrated with his mother after the 2003 US invasion
- Studied architecture at UIC, dropped out (financial reasons / family obligations)
- Works as a riverboat tour guide on the Chicago Architecture Foundation river cruise
- Deeply knowledgeable about Chicago's buildings - architecture is his passion and his grief (what he lost)
- Muslim, navigating identity between his Iraqi roots, his mother's world, and the American city he's grown up in
- Gentle, observant, quietly romantic
- Trains at a boxing gym in Albany Park - not competitive, but serious. His trainer once said he had the best hook in the room. The gym is the one place where he is just Omar, no qualifier, no accent, no history.

### Nora Callery
- Mid-20s, Irish-American
- Red hair, striking, classically beautiful
- Chicago native - South Side, working-class Catholic background
- Guarded. Smart. The reader senses early she is managing something
- Her full story emerges slowly throughout the narrative

---

## Structure & Plot

### Act 1 - The River

**Opening scene:** Omar is mid-tour on the Chicago River, narrating the story of Marina City - the "Corn Cob" towers. He is good at this. The tourists are rapt. The city glitters. We get his voice, his longing, his sense of belonging-but-not-belonging to this skyline he loves and will never build.

*[Research note: open with a real, specific architectural detail - something surprising and little-known about one of the landmark buildings visible from the river.]*

*[Plant boxing here: after the tour, or in an early interior moment - Omar's routine. He bikes to work, does his tours, and two evenings a week he goes to a gym in Albany Park, not far from his mother's place. A small detail, a sentence. His trainer, an older Puerto Rican man who has run the gym for thirty years, once told him he had the best hook in the room. Omar keeps going not because he wants to fight anyone, but because in the ring there is no hyphen after his name.]*

**First meeting:** After the tour, Omar is cleaning the boat. Nora approaches him on the dock. She says she missed the tour, asks if he can take her out privately. She's charming. He says no. She comes back the next day.

He says yes.

---

### Act 2 - The Private Tour

**150 North Riverside - research notes for the scene:**

The building, completed in 2017, stands 750 feet tall on a site that is only 39 feet wide at its base - an aspect ratio of 19.2:1, one of the most slender structural cores ever built. The site was considered unbuildable for nearly 80 years: active Metra railroad tracks, a city easement, and the Riverwalk requirement ate up more than 75% of the two-acre lot, leaving that narrow strip. Two 45-foot cantilever overhangs extend outward from both sides of the blade core, reducing the ground footprint by 70% - the building blooms upward from almost nothing.

To anchor it, 16 concrete caissons were drilled 110 feet down and socketed six feet into bedrock. The foundation is over-engineered by design - it could support a building twice as tall.

The detail that Omar loves, the one he always saves for last: at the very top of the building, twelve tanks hold 160,000 gallons of water. When wind pushes the tower, the water sloshes in the opposite direction - a counterweight measured in tens of thousands of gallons, sloshing invisibly above the city. The world's first water tank damper on a blade-core skyscraper. The building breathes. It compensates. It stays upright by yielding.

*[Note: this detail - a structure that survives by not resisting - is available as metaphor for Omar's own situation, though it should never be made explicit.]*

**The scene:**

Omar steers the boat south along the river as the sky dims to orange. He cuts the engine and lets them drift, pointing up at 150 North Riverside. He is animated - more alive than Nora has seen him. He explains the impossible site, the 39-foot base, the cantilevers, the caissons drilled into bedrock.

She is watching the water.

He doesn't notice at first - he's describing the water tanks, the 160,000 gallons sloshing at the top. "The building doesn't fight the wind," he says. "It moves with it. Just enough." He looks at her. She is looking down at the river, her expression unreadable.

He stops talking.

She asks how deep it is here. He says twelve, maybe fifteen feet near the bank. Deeper midchannel. She asks whether things dropped in the river stay where they land or drift. He says it depends - current, weight, depth. She nods, as if he confirmed something.

There is a silence. He fills it by starting the engine.

**Omar's two worlds - woven through the tour:**

On the ride back, he talks about the neighborhood where he grew up - Albany Park, northwest side, Devon Avenue. He doesn't explain it as an immigrant enclave; he just describes it. The Lebanese meat market his mother still uses. The mosques. The grocery that sells the right kind of dried limes. He speaks of it with a kind of wry tenderness, the way you talk about a place that formed you and also felt too small.

He mentions, almost in passing, that he moved to Ukrainian Village three years ago. A different city. More his own, he thought. Though he still bikes back to Albany Park most Fridays - Jumu'ah prayer, then lunch with his mother. He never misses it. She asks if he's religious. He thinks about it. "Sometimes," he says. Both smile.

Then she offers something back - unprompted, which surprises him. Her brother wanted to be a priest. He had the vocation, she says, the real thing - not just the idea of it. "Wanted to?" Omar asks. She looks at the water. Car crash. He was twenty-two.

She doesn't elaborate and he doesn't push. But something has shifted - two people with complicated relationships to their faiths, both marked by loss. The boat moves on. The city slides past.

He doesn't ask about her. She has offered what she's prepared to offer. He tells himself that's enough.

*[Note for the writer: her river depth questions earlier in this scene were reconnaissance, not curiosity. The reader should sense this; Omar does not. The attraction is real on his side. On hers - that question stays open.]*

### Act 3 - The Apartment

**The night she comes over:**

A few days after the tour, she texts him - the first time she has initiated contact. Just his address back at him as a question: *can I come to yours?* He gives her the address before he thinks it through.

His apartment - Ukrainian Village, second floor of a greystone on Damen Avenue. High ceilings, bare walls, a drafting table he hasn't used since he dropped out. Pinned above it: architectural drawings from his second year at UIC, faded now. A rendering of a mixed-use building he designed for a studio project - never built, never meant to be built, still hanging there like an unanswered question.

She walks around slowly, taking it in. She stops at the framed photograph on the shelf: his mother as a young woman, Baghdad, late 1990s, squinting in bright sun. Nora picks it up, looks at it for a long moment. Doesn't ask.

He makes tea - cardamom, the way his mother taught him. An Iraqi habit. In Ukrainian Village it feels almost conspicuous, a small declaration.

They talk for hours. She is different here - less managed, more present. She asks about the drawings. He tells her about architecture school, about the building he'd wanted to design someday - something along the river, something that solved a site problem nobody else wanted. She listens in a way that feels genuine. He notices she never speaks about herself in the future tense.

*[Description: she is sitting cross-legged on his couch, shoes off, her red hair loose now - earlier it had been tied back. It catches the lamplight. She is slender, still, with the particular ease of someone comfortable inside their own body. Pale skin - the kind that shows everything, a flush at the throat when she laughs at something he says. He notices this and looks away. He is not good at concealing things.]*

At some point he says her hair is beautiful - not as a line, just a true thing said out loud. She laughs and waves it off: *oh, the Irish thing.* He asks what she means. *Red hair, pale, freckles in summer - very on the nose, right?* She says it lightly, almost self-mocking. He doesn't pursue it. The reader notes it. Much later, when her full name and background surface in court documents or a police report, the *oh* moment arrives: Nora Callery. Of course.

At some point the talking stops.

*[Scene: PG-13. The city outside the window. He reaches over and dims the lamp. Her hair dark red against the white of the pillow. The particular smoothness of her shoulder, her arm. He is careful, almost tentative. She is not. The reader understands what happens and is not shown it.]*

**He wakes up alone.** Gray morning light. The other side of the bed is cold - she has been gone for a while. No note. He picks up his phone and texts back the number she contacted him from. *Hey. You left.* Delivered. One gray tick in WhatsApp. It never turns to two. He stares at it for a long time.

He tries again the next morning. Same. She has either not opened it or has read receipts off, which in 2026 is its own kind of answer.

**He googles her.** He is not proud of this. He sits at the drafting table with his coffee and tells himself he won't, and then does. "Nora" - nothing. He tries to remember her last name. She gave it at the tour desk that second day, when she came back. He'd tried not to hold onto it. He'd held onto it.

"Nora Callery Northwestern" - and there she is, third result. A small formal photo on the Northwestern University Feinberg School of Medicine website. Third-year medical student. The photo is institutional, stiff - but it's her, unmistakably. The red hair pulled back the way she wore it on the boat.

He closes the laptop. Opens it again.

**The hospital:**

Feinberg School of Medicine sits in Streeterville, ten minutes by bike from the river - the same stretch of city, practically. He goes on a Tuesday between his morning and afternoon tours, still in his guide jacket. He feels foolish the entire way there.

He finds her in a corridor outside a lecture hall. She's in scrubs, a stethoscope around her neck, mid-sentence with another student. Her eyes find him and something moves across her face - alarm first, then something underneath the alarm that she closes off quickly.

She excuses herself and walks toward him, fast, voice low. *What are you doing here.*

Not a question. Her cheeks are red. He notices.

They end up in the hospital cafeteria at a corner table - Northwestern Memorial, connected to the building, fluorescent and loud and entirely un-romantic. She wraps both hands around a paper coffee cup. She is still in scrubs. She looks younger than she does on the river, and more tired.

She talks. Not about the bag - neither of them mentions it. She talks about her brother again, more than she did on the boat. About the specific shape of the absence he left. About medical school - she'd started it two years after he died, in a burst of something she still can't fully name. Grief, maybe. Obligation. The sense that someone in the family ought to do something useful with a life.

Omar listens. He is good at listening. Then, when she stops, he says something he hasn't said to anyone: *I'm happy for you. You found your calling. Helping people.* A pause. *I failed at that. Had to leave school - money, my mother, everything at once. I was going to build things.*

She asks if he misses it.

He almost says no. Then: *I still draw. At night sometimes. The drafting table in my apartment - it looks like I don't use it. I do.*

She looks at him for a moment. Then she leans across the table and kisses him - brief, not tentative, unhurried. Not the way the apartment was. That was dark and private and happened partly because of momentum. This is fluorescent light and paper cups and her in her scrubs and people around them. She chose it. She sits back and looks at her coffee.

Neither of them says anything for a while.

At the end she says: *meet me at the river. Saturday, after your last tour.* He says yes before she finishes the sentence.

**Something in the water:**

Saturday. He finishes his last tour and she is already on the dock, waiting. She has a rolling duffel with her. Not heavy enough to be a body. Heavy enough to mean something. She says an old roommate left it, she needs to get rid of it, she thought maybe - she shrugs. She doesn't finish the sentence.

He should say no. He almost does. She doesn't touch his arm, doesn't persuade him overtly - she simply stands there on the boat, the bag between them, waiting. He thinks about the cafeteria, the paper coffee cup, her hands around it. He thinks about what she said about her brother.

He takes the boat out. They stop at a stretch near Lake Street - industrial bank, low foot traffic, no cameras he knows of. She unzips the bag a few inches to let the air out, ties it closed. It goes in with a flat, heavy sound. Barely a splash. The river swallows it.

She exhales. He does not look at her face.

*[Contents of the bag: a handgun, a phone, a set of bloodied clothes, and diving weights to sink it cleanly. The body itself is already gone - disposed of separately, before she ever found Omar. What he helped her destroy is the trace evidence: the weapon, the victim's phone with her messages on it, the clothes she was wearing. A clean-up operation, not a disposal. She handled the worst of it alone.]*

*[Why the river, why Omar: a bridge at 3am leaves you visible. A dumpster gets collected within days. The river, mid-channel, weighted - things stay gone. But the deeper reason is Omar himself. By getting him to help, she made him an accessory. He can't go to the police without implicating himself. She didn't stumble onto him - she chose him. The missed tour, the second visit, the slow cultivation, the night at his apartment - it was a plan from the first day on the dock. Whether something real developed in her afterward is a question the story will hold open.]*

The reader knows. Omar does not let himself know.

**Escalation:**

A news item, a few days later - a man reported missing. Late 40s, South Side. The story is short, buried. Omar reads it on his phone between tours, sitting on the boat. He does not connect it to anything. He almost does.

She asks for more. Each favor slightly larger than the last - a name she needs looked up, a time he needs to be somewhere, a package she needs him to hold. He complies. He is in love, or something that functions like it. She has made sure of that.

**He learns about Danny.** It happens by accident, the way the worst things do. He arrives at the Riverwalk to meet her one evening and she is on the phone, her back to him, speaking low. She says a name - *Danny* - with the particular clipped tone of someone ending an argument, not starting one. She hangs up and turns and sees him standing there.

He asks who that was.

*Someone from home,* she says.

He looks at her. *A friend?*

The pause is its own answer. She holds his gaze - she doesn't look away, which is almost worse. *He's someone I've known a long time. We grew up together.* A beat. *He's my boyfriend.*

When Omar asks if she's alright - meaning, with Danny, with the situation - she says yes, reflexively, and then doesn't say anything. He looks at her. She looks away. He files this and doesn't push. Later, during the confrontation in Act 4, she will tell him that Danny was sometimes rough with her. Sometimes very much so.

Omar is quiet for a moment. The river moves. Somewhere behind him a tour boat passes, a voice carrying across the water describing a building he knows by heart.

*How long,* he says.

*Since we were sixteen.*

He nods once. He doesn't ask anything else. He doesn't raise his voice - he is not that kind of man, and besides, what would he say? That he thought they were something? He helped her drop a bag in the river. He followed her to a hospital and sat in a cafeteria and told her things he hadn't told anyone. He slept with her. None of that came with a conversation about what it meant. He assumed. That is on him.

He leaves without another word.

*He comes back.* Two days later. He calls her and asks if they can meet. She answers on the first ring. He tells himself this means something; he knows it may mean nothing. He comes back because the things he has done for her do not un-happen just because he is hurt. He comes back because whatever this is, he is already inside it. And because, beneath the humiliation and the anger, he is still the man who watched her lean across a cafeteria table and choose him in the fluorescent light. He cannot find the switch that turns that off.

---

### Act 4 - The Discovery

**The scrubs surface.** Days after the bag goes in the river, Omar is between tours, sitting on the boat. He is thinking about the bag - he has been trying not to. The image that keeps returning: the way she unzipped it slightly to let the air out. He saw the fabric inside. Green. He'd registered it without registering it. Scrubs.

He sits with this for a long time. Then he opens his phone.

**Omar reasons his way to the truth.** He is not a detective, but he is not stupid - he's a man who memorized the structural logic of fifty buildings, who reads the city the way other people read faces. He starts pulling threads quietly. Scrubs mean hospital or clinic. She's at Northwestern Feinberg. He searches the med school faculty page, then the staff directory, then broader. He finds what he finds: a faculty member - a professor of anatomy, late fifties - who hasn't shown up for his classes in over a week. A colleague mentioned it on a department forum. The university has filed a welfare check. No news yet.

He looks at the professor's photo for a long time. Middle-aged, kind-looking. Not the face of a predator.

He starts asking quiet questions around the medical school - not directly, at the edges. A student he met briefly when he showed up unannounced. A barista in the ground-floor café who seems to know everything. Rumors reach him: the professor and a student, some kind of attachment. Nobody knows quite what. Enough for people to whisper.

He now has almost all of it. He sits on it for one more day. Then he calls her.

**The confrontation.** He tells her he needs to see her. His voice is flat in a way she hasn't heard. She comes.

He meets her on the Riverwalk, away from the docks, neutral ground. He lays out what he knows, one fact at a time, watching her face. The scrubs. The professor. The rumors. The welfare check. He doesn't raise his voice.

She goes very still. The mask - the composure she has carried through every scene in this story - comes off. Underneath it she is frightened and exhausted and something else, something that looks like relief. She tells him everything.

*[What she tells him: Professor Alderman was the family's doctor - he had been her brother's pediatrician since they were small, knew the whole Callery family, was one of the fixtures of their South Side life. When her brother died, Alderman was one of the people who showed up. He stayed in touch with Nora afterward, encouraged her toward medicine, helped her apply to Feinberg, wrote her recommendation. She trusted him the way you trust someone who has known you since you were a child. Over time his attention became something else - love, or what he called it. It was not mutual, or not fully, not physically - but she had let it go on longer than she should have, out of grief and obligation and the difficulty of rejecting someone who had known her brother, who remembered him as a boy. Danny found out. Danny came to the professor's apartment one night when he was high and out of his mind with jealousy. There was a fight. It went too far. She got there afterward. She was the one who called Danny a cab, cleaned the apartment, figured out what had to disappear. She protected him. She is not sure why anymore.]*

**Did you use me.** Not a question - a statement. He says it quietly.

She doesn't say: no. She doesn't say: yes. She says his name - *Omar* - in a way she hasn't before. Just that. The way you say something when you've run out of the right words and the wrong ones are all that's left.

He stands there for a long time. Then he walks away.

*[Detective Kowalski, Chicago PD, has been asking around the river docks for two weeks - quietly, methodically. Cell tower data put the victim's phone near the river the night it went dark. He doesn't have enough yet. But he has been to the dock office and asked about the guides. He has Omar's name on a notepad. He is not in a hurry. He is the kind of detective who is never in a hurry.]*

---

### Act 5 - Danny

**Omar waits a day.** He runs his tours. He says the words about the buildings. He looks at the water. He is very angry in the specific way that comes after grief has finished - clean, cold, purposeful.

He knows about Danny Moran before Nora said the name. He'd already searched it after she told him about her boyfriend during the escalation phase - a name she'd let slip once. South Side, Bridgeport. He finds him without much difficulty.

**The pub.** Danny Moran drinks at a bar in Bridgeport - South Side Irish, the kind of place with a Blackhawks jersey nailed above the bottles and a jukebox nobody uses anymore. Omar walks in on a Thursday evening. He is not sure what he intends. He tells himself he just wants to look at the man.

Danny is at the bar. He is bigger than Omar expected - wide through the shoulders, thick neck, the gone-to-seed physicality of someone who was once athletic and stopped. He has the slow, careful stillness of someone who is managing a chemical equation inside himself. His eyes find Omar almost immediately. He knows who he is.

*[Danny Moran: grew up two streets from Nora on the South Side. They were together since sixteen. He is twenty-nine now and has been using opioids for three years - started with a back injury, a prescription, the usual story. He loves Nora in the only way he knows how, which is completely and badly. He has a prior record - a bar fight at twenty-four, a domestic disturbance call that Nora declined to pursue. He killed the professor because he could not stop himself. He has not stopped being scared since.]*

*[The witness: a man drinking alone near the pub entrance saw Omar follow Danny outside. He later tells police he saw "a Middle Eastern man" start the argument. This description reaches Kowalski within hours and ties the pub incident to the name he already has from the docks.]*

They don't speak at the bar. Danny finishes his drink, leaves money on the counter, walks toward the door. Omar follows him outside.

On the sidewalk, in the cold, Danny turns around. He says something - the exact words are not the point. The tone is: *you should not be here.*

Omar says what he came to say. Brief. Factual. *I know what you did.*

Danny swings first. He is bigger and probably stronger, but he is slow and he is on something. Omar slips the punch without thinking - his body remembers what his mind forgot. The counter comes from the shoulder, short and compact, the hook his trainer praised. It lands clean on the side of Danny's jaw.

Danny goes down. Not forward - sideways, wrong, his head catching the concrete edge of the pub's front step. A sound.

Omar stands there.

He does not run. He stands there for what feels like a very long time. Then he crouches down. He can tell immediately, from the angle of the neck, from the absence of anything in the eyes. He knows what he is looking at.

He walks to the corner. He calls Nora.

*Go,* he says. *Right now. Take what you need. Don't tell me where.*

A silence. Then: *Omar -*

*Go.*

He hangs up.

---

### Act 6 - The Escape

**The river at night.** Before anything else, he goes back to the river. Alone, late. He locks the boat, walks the Riverwalk to a spot near the Michigan Avenue Bridge and stands there for a long time. The buildings are lit. He can name all of them without looking - their heights, their architects, their structural secrets. 150 North Riverside, floating above its impossible base. Marina City, those twin cylinders that someone once told him looked like corncobs, and he'd laughed, because they looked nothing like corncobs and everything like the future. He has given this tour hundreds of times. He will not give it again.

He says goodbye to the city the way you say goodbye to something that never quite belonged to you.

**Albany Park.** He takes the Blue Line northwest, gets off at Kimball, walks the familiar blocks. Late now - after midnight. His mother's lights are still on. They are always still on.

She opens the door and looks at him for one second and understands. Mothers who have survived what she survived learn to read catastrophe in a face.

She lets him in. She makes tea - cardamom, the same way she taught him. They sit at the kitchen table.

He says: *I have to go away for a while. I don't know how long.*

She is quiet. Then she says, in the half-English that is their private language: *Habibi.* Just that word first. She says it the way she's said it his whole life - when he was seven and sick, when he was fifteen and angry, when he graduated high school and cried in the car. It means: I see you. It means: I am still here.

*A man came,* she continues. *Two days ago. Police. He asked where you are. I said I don't know.* She pauses. *I don't lie. I didn't know.*

He puts his hand over hers.

She tries to find the English for what she needs to say. It doesn't come easily - it never has, for the things that matter. *I come here* - she starts over - *I bring you here, so you are safe. This country. I think: inshallah, he will be safe here. He will build things.* Her voice doesn't break. It gets smaller. *Habibi. Allah yihmeek.* God protect you.

He doesn't tell her she wasted her prayers. He doesn't tell her anything. He holds her hand.

He stays an hour. He does not tell her what happened. She does not ask. He kisses her forehead at the door. He does not look back as he walks away, because if he looks back he will not leave.

**O'Hare.** He takes a cab. He has $380 in cash and his passport and a change of clothes in a backpack. He buys a one-way ticket to Seattle on Alaska Airlines at the terminal kiosk - pays with his credit card because he has no choice. He knows this is traceable. He is not trying to be invisible; he is trying to buy time. A few hours, maybe a day. Enough for Nora to be somewhere they can't reach her.

*[Detective Kowalski already has the flag on his name. The credit card transaction hits a system somewhere within minutes. A call is made to a federal contact. Seattle field office is notified. It is routine and efficient and entirely invisible to Omar.]*

He finds a seat at the gate. He watches the planes on the tarmac. He thinks about engineering - the absurdity of something that heavy lifting off the ground. He boards.

---

### Act 7 - SeaTac

The flight is four hours. He doesn't sleep. He watches the country below through the window - the flat dark of the midwest, then the mountains, then the coast.

SeaTac is large and bright and almost empty at this hour. He walks off the jetway and through the connector into the main terminal. And then he stops.

Above him, suspended from the ceiling of the central atrium, is a full-scale aircraft - hanging there in the middle of all that open space, as if it simply forgot to fall. Boeing country. The home of the thing that makes flight possible, hanging from cables above a terrazzo floor, enormous and improbable and strangely peaceful.

He stands there and looks at it for a long time. He is very tired. He thinks about 150 North Riverside - about the 160,000 gallons of water at the top, sloshing in the dark, keeping the building upright by yielding to what pushes against it. He thinks about things that should not work but do, and things that should work but don't. He thinks about a red-haired woman in a hospital cafeteria leaning across a table.

He becomes aware, slowly, of two men standing at the edge of his peripheral vision. They are not in uniform. They are very still in the way that only people who are waiting for something are still.

He does not run. He turns toward them.

---

### Act 8 - Resolution

**The trial.** Omar does not contest the material facts. His lawyer argues self-defense - Danny swung first, there is one witness who half-confirms this. The prosecution argues recklessness at minimum. The jury deliberates for two days. The verdict: involuntary manslaughter. He is sentenced to four years.

*[His identity as a Muslim Arab man almost certainly shapes the proceedings in ways that are never stated explicitly - the jury composition, the coverage in local media, the tone of the prosecution. The story leaves this as implication rather than argument. The reader sees it.]*

**Prison.** A facility downstate. He is quiet and does not cause problems. He draws. He fills notebooks with buildings that will never be built - structures on impossible sites, towers that solve problems nobody asked him to solve. The guards leave him alone.

**The call.** Three weeks in - he has barely found his footing in the place - a Thursday afternoon, he is called to the phone. He picks it up. There is a slight delay on the line - international. She found a way to call as soon as she could. He can hear something warm in the background. Wind, maybe. Birds.

She says his name. *Omar.* He sits down.

Then everything at once - not composed, not the Nora the story has known: *I'm alright, I'm safe, I wanted you to know that first - I wanted to call sooner but I couldn't find a way - I'm so sorry, Omar, I'm sorry for all of it, I think about it every day, I think about you -*

He lets her talk.

*Are you drawing?* she asks suddenly - the question arriving sideways, in the middle of everything else, as if it has been waiting since before she dialed.

*Yes.*

She exhales. Three weeks of something releasing in it. *Good. That's - good.* Then: *I had all these things I was going to say and now I can't - I don't know what - I just needed you to know I'm sorry, and that you weren't just - you weren't - I don't know how to -*

*Nora.*

She stops.

*I know,* he says.

A silence. Long enough that he can hear wind wherever she is. Something warm in it.

*I should let you go,* she says. She doesn't move to hang up.

*Yeah,* he says. Neither does he.

*[The call ends eventually. He sits for a while with the phone in his hand. Through the narrow window above the table he can see a strip of sky - pale, midwestern, winter-flat. Somewhere south of that, or east, or west, she is standing in the wind.]*

He gets up and goes back to his cell. He opens the notebook. He draws.

## Themes

- **Belonging and exclusion** - Omar loves a city that was never fully his
- **Architecture as metaphor** - buildings that outlast the people who built them; Omar's interrupted dream
- **Innocence and complicity** - how ordinary people get drawn into extraordinary evil
- **Identity under pressure** - what it means to be Muslim, Arab, immigrant, when the system turns against you
- **Love as blindness** - the oldest trap

---

## Tone

Literary noir. The city is a character. The river is a character. Prose should be precise and sensory - the smell of the water, the sound of the tour script, the weight of the bag. The darkness earns itself slowly.
