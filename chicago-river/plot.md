# Chicago River - Plot

## Genre
Noir thriller / literary fiction

---

## Characters

### Omar Al-Rashidi
- 27 years old
- Born in Baghdad, emigrated with his mother after the 2003 US invasion
- Studied architecture at UIC, dropped out (financial reasons / family obligations)
- Works as a riverboat tour guide on the Chicago Architecture Foundation river cruise
- Deeply knowledgeable about Chicago's buildings - architecture is his passion and his grief (what he lost)
- Muslim, navigating identity between his Iraqi roots, his mother's world, and the American city he's grown up in
- Gentle, observant, quietly romantic

### Nora Callery
- Mid-20s, Irish-American
- Red hair, striking, classically beautiful
- Chicago native - South Side, working-class Catholic background
- Guarded. Smart. The reader senses early she is managing something
- Her full story emerges slowly throughout the narrative

---

## Structure & Plot

### Act 1 - The River

**Opening scene:** Omar is mid-tour on the Chicago River, narrating the story of Marina City - the "Corn Cob" towers. He is good at this. The tourists are rapt. The city glitters. We get his voice, his longing, his sense of belonging-but-not-belonging to this skyline he loves and will never build.

*[Research note: open with a real, specific architectural detail - something surprising and little-known about one of the landmark buildings visible from the river.]*

**First meeting:** After the tour, Omar is cleaning the boat. Nora approaches him on the dock. She says she missed the tour, asks if he can take her out privately. She's charming. He says no. She comes back the next day.

He says yes.

### Act 2 - The Private Tour

**150 North Riverside - research notes for the scene:**

The building, completed in 2017, stands 750 feet tall on a site that is only 39 feet wide at its base - an aspect ratio of 19.2:1, one of the most slender structural cores ever built. The site was considered unbuildable for nearly 80 years: active Metra railroad tracks, a city easement, and the Riverwalk requirement ate up more than 75% of the two-acre lot, leaving that narrow strip. Two 45-foot cantilever overhangs extend outward from both sides of the blade core, reducing the ground footprint by 70% - the building blooms upward from almost nothing.

To anchor it, 16 concrete caissons were drilled 110 feet down and socketed six feet into bedrock. The foundation is over-engineered by design - it could support a building twice as tall.

The detail that Omar loves, the one he always saves for last: at the very top of the building, twelve tanks hold 160,000 gallons of water. When wind pushes the tower, the water sloshes in the opposite direction - a counterweight measured in tens of thousands of gallons, sloshing invisibly above the city. The world's first water tank damper on a blade-core skyscraper. The building breathes. It compensates. It stays upright by yielding.

*[Note: this detail - a structure that survives by not resisting - is available as metaphor for Omar's own situation, though it should never be made explicit.]*

**The scene:**

Omar steers the boat south along the river as the sky dims to orange. He cuts the engine and lets them drift, pointing up at 150 North Riverside. He is animated - more alive than Nora has seen him. He explains the impossible site, the 39-foot base, the cantilevers, the caissons drilled into bedrock.

She is watching the water.

He doesn't notice at first - he's describing the water tanks, the 160,000 gallons sloshing at the top. "The building doesn't fight the wind," he says. "It moves with it. Just enough." He looks at her. She is looking down at the river, her expression unreadable.

He stops talking.

She asks how deep it is here. He says twelve, maybe fifteen feet near the bank. Deeper midchannel. She asks whether things dropped in the river stay where they land or drift. He says it depends - current, weight, depth. She nods, as if he confirmed something.

There is a silence. He fills it by starting the engine.

**Omar's two worlds - woven through the tour:**

On the ride back, he talks about the neighborhood where he grew up - Albany Park, northwest side, Devon Avenue. He doesn't explain it as an immigrant enclave; he just describes it. The Lebanese meat market his mother still uses. The mosques. The grocery that sells the right kind of dried limes. He speaks of it with a kind of wry tenderness, the way you talk about a place that formed you and also felt too small.

He mentions, almost in passing, that he moved to Ukrainian Village three years ago. A different city. More his own, he thought. Though he still bikes back to Albany Park most Fridays - Jumu'ah prayer, then lunch with his mother. He never misses it. She asks if he's religious. He thinks about it. "Sometimes," he says. Both smile.

Then she offers something back - unprompted, which surprises him. Her brother wanted to be a priest. He had the vocation, she says, the real thing - not just the idea of it. "Wanted to?" Omar asks. She looks at the water. Car crash. He was twenty-two.

She doesn't elaborate and he doesn't push. But something has shifted - two people with complicated relationships to their faiths, both marked by loss. The boat moves on. The city slides past.

He doesn't ask about her. She has offered what she's prepared to offer. He tells himself that's enough.

*[Note for the writer: her river depth questions earlier in this scene were reconnaissance, not curiosity. The reader should sense this; Omar does not. The attraction is real on his side. On hers - that question stays open.]*

### Act 3 - The Apartment

**The night she comes over:**

A few days after the tour, she texts him - the first time she has initiated contact. Just his address back at him as a question: *can I come to yours?* He gives her the address before he thinks it through.

His apartment - Ukrainian Village, second floor of a greystone on Damen Avenue. High ceilings, bare walls, a drafting table he hasn't used since he dropped out. Pinned above it: architectural drawings from his second year at UIC, faded now. A rendering of a mixed-use building he designed for a studio project - never built, never meant to be built, still hanging there like an unanswered question.

She walks around slowly, taking it in. She stops at the framed photograph on the shelf: his mother as a young woman, Baghdad, late 1990s, squinting in bright sun. Nora picks it up, looks at it for a long moment. Doesn't ask.

He makes tea - cardamom, the way his mother taught him. An Iraqi habit. In Ukrainian Village it feels almost conspicuous, a small declaration.

They talk for hours. She is different here - less managed, more present. She asks about the drawings. He tells her about architecture school, about the building he'd wanted to design someday - something along the river, something that solved a site problem nobody else wanted. She listens in a way that feels genuine. He notices she never speaks about herself in the future tense.

*[Description: she is sitting cross-legged on his couch, shoes off, her red hair loose now - earlier it had been tied back. It catches the lamplight. She is slender, still, with the particular ease of someone comfortable inside their own body. Pale skin - the kind that shows everything, a flush at the throat when she laughs at something he says. He notices this and looks away. He is not good at concealing things.]*

At some point the talking stops.

*[Scene: PG-13. The city outside the window. He reaches over and dims the lamp. Her hair dark red against the white of the pillow. The particular smoothness of her shoulder, her arm. He is careful, almost tentative. She is not. The reader understands what happens and is not shown it.]*

**He wakes up alone.** Gray morning light. The other side of the bed is cold - she has been gone for a while. No note. He picks up his phone and texts back the number she contacted him from. *Hey. You left.* Delivered. One gray tick in WhatsApp. It never turns to two. He stares at it for a long time.

He tries again the next morning. Same. She has either not opened it or has read receipts off, which in 2026 is its own kind of answer.

**He googles her.** He is not proud of this. He sits at the drafting table with his coffee and tells himself he won't, and then does. "Nora" - nothing. He tries to remember her last name. She gave it at the tour desk that second day, when she came back. He'd tried not to hold onto it. He'd held onto it.

"Nora Callery Northwestern" - and there she is, third result. A small formal photo on the Northwestern University Feinberg School of Medicine website. Third-year medical student. The photo is institutional, stiff - but it's her, unmistakably. The red hair pulled back the way she wore it on the boat.

He closes the laptop. Opens it again.

**The hospital:**

Feinberg School of Medicine sits in Streeterville, ten minutes by bike from the river - the same stretch of city, practically. He goes on a Tuesday between his morning and afternoon tours, still in his guide jacket. He feels foolish the entire way there.

He finds her in a corridor outside a lecture hall. She's in scrubs, a stethoscope around her neck, mid-sentence with another student. Her eyes find him and something moves across her face - alarm first, then something underneath the alarm that she closes off quickly.

She excuses herself and walks toward him, fast, voice low. *What are you doing here.*

Not a question. Her cheeks are red. He notices.

They end up in the hospital cafeteria at a corner table - Northwestern Memorial, connected to the building, fluorescent and loud and entirely un-romantic. She wraps both hands around a paper coffee cup. She is still in scrubs. She looks younger than she does on the river, and more tired.

She talks. Not about the bag - neither of them mentions it. She talks about her brother again, more than she did on the boat. About the specific shape of the absence he left. About medical school - she'd started it two years after he died, in a burst of something she still can't fully name. Grief, maybe. Obligation. The sense that someone in the family ought to do something useful with a life.

Omar listens. He is good at listening.

At the end she says: *meet me at the river. Saturday, after your last tour.* He says yes before she finishes the sentence.

**Something in the water:**

Saturday. He finishes his last tour and she is already on the dock, waiting. She has a rolling duffel with her. Not heavy enough to be a body. Heavy enough to mean something. She says an old roommate left it, she needs to get rid of it, she thought maybe - she shrugs. She doesn't finish the sentence.

He should say no. He almost does. She doesn't touch his arm, doesn't persuade him overtly - she simply stands there on the boat, the bag between them, waiting. He thinks about the cafeteria, the paper coffee cup, her hands around it. He thinks about what she said about her brother.

He takes the boat out. They stop at a stretch near Lake Street - industrial bank, low foot traffic, no cameras he knows of. She unzips the bag a few inches to let the air out, ties it closed. It goes in with a flat, heavy sound. Barely a splash. The river swallows it.

She exhales. He does not look at her face.

*[Contents of the bag: a handgun, a phone, a set of bloodied clothes, and diving weights to sink it cleanly. The body itself is already gone - disposed of separately, before she ever found Omar. What he helped her destroy is the trace evidence: the weapon, the victim's phone with her messages on it, the clothes she was wearing. A clean-up operation, not a disposal. She handled the worst of it alone.]*

*[Why the river, why Omar: a bridge at 3am leaves you visible. A dumpster gets collected within days. The river, mid-channel, weighted - things stay gone. But the deeper reason is Omar himself. By getting him to help, she made him an accessory. He can't go to the police without implicating himself. She didn't stumble onto him - she chose him. The missed tour, the second visit, the slow cultivation, the night at his apartment - it was a plan from the first day on the dock. Whether something real developed in her afterward is a question the story will hold open.]*

The reader knows. Omar does not let himself know.

**Escalation:**

A news item, a few days later - a man reported missing. Late 30s, South Side. The story is short, buried. Omar reads it on his phone between tours, sitting on the boat. He does not connect it to anything. He almost does.

She asks for more. Each favor slightly larger than the last - a name she needs looked up, a time he needs to be somewhere, a package she needs him to hold. He complies. He is in love, or something that functions like it. She has made sure of that.

### Act 4 - The Point of No Return

**The cop:** A Chicago PD detective starts asking questions around the river docks. He's been watching Omar. One night, he corners Omar alone - on the boat, after hours. He knows something. He may be corrupt (connected to whoever Nora is running from), or he may be legitimate - this is deliberately ambiguous.

**Omar kills the cop.** It is not premeditated. It happens fast. The circumstances make it feel both inevitable and horrifying - the reader understands how he got here even as they recoil.

**The aftermath:** Omar is now a fugitive. He is alone. His mother doesn't know. Nora has gone quiet.

## Themes

- **Belonging and exclusion** - Omar loves a city that was never fully his
- **Architecture as metaphor** - buildings that outlast the people who built them; Omar's interrupted dream
- **Innocence and complicity** - how ordinary people get drawn into extraordinary evil
- **Identity under pressure** - what it means to be Muslim, Arab, immigrant, when the system turns against you
- **Love as blindness** - the oldest trap

---

## Tone

Literary noir. The city is a character. The river is a character. Prose should be precise and sensory - the smell of the water, the sound of the tour script, the weight of the bag. The darkness earns itself slowly.
