# The Chicago River

---

## I.

The river was reversed once.

Omar always started with this - not with the skyline or the bridges or the green water catching the afternoon light, but with that single fact, which was the strangest thing about Chicago and therefore the thing he loved most about it.

"Eighteen ninety-nine," he said, his voice carrying across the boat. "Chicago's engineers reversed the flow of the entire river. Not a canal. Not a side channel. The river itself. They sent it backward - away from Lake Michigan, where the city had been dumping its waste for fifty years." He let that land. "Fourteen thousand men. Two years. They moved a river."

Twenty-two tourists on a Wednesday afternoon in October, the city bright and cold on both banks. The Wrigley Building pale as bone to the north, the Tribune Tower with its fragments of famous buildings pressed into its base like stolen relics.

"Why does that matter?" He didn't wait for an answer. "Because every building you see from this boat exists because somebody decided the world should be different than it was. They didn't ask the river's permission."

He was good at this. Three years in, he knew where to pause, where to let the city speak for itself, where to bring it back in. The tourists who were really listening - there were always two or three - went home with something they hadn't expected. The rest got their photos and that was fine too.

He noticed the woman at the front rail. Red hair, tied back, dark jacket. She'd boarded last and chosen to stand rather than sit. He noticed because guides notice who's paying attention and who isn't. She was watching the water more than the skyline. He noted it and moved on.

---

He docked at four-fifteen, secured the lines, started hosing down the deck. The sky had gone the particular flat gray that meant November was thinking about arriving early.

Two evenings a week he had somewhere to be: a boxing gym in Albany Park, three blocks from his mother's building, run by a man named Reyes who had been training fighters in that same room for thirty years. Reyes had the patience of someone who expected to be disappointed and occasionally wasn't. Omar had started going at sixteen, when he'd needed somewhere to put the anger he didn't have words for. He wasn't a fighter - Reyes had told him that plainly, not unkindly. *You think too much in the ring. But that hook -* He'd shaken his head. *That hook could end someone's evening.*

Omar hadn't hit anyone in four years. He kept going anyway. In the gym there was no qualifier after his name. He was just the guy with the hook. That was enough.

He was coiling the hose when he heard someone behind him on the dock.

"Tours are done for today," he said, without turning.

"I know. I missed it."

He turned. The woman from the rail. Up close she was striking in a way he hadn't fully registered from the helm - the red hair, very pale skin, something still and watchful in the way she stood.

"Heading back out tomorrow at ten," he said.

"I was hoping for something a little more private."

"We don't really do that."

"I'll pay."

"Still no."

She looked at him for a moment - steady, not embarrassed. "Okay." She turned and walked back up the dock.

He watched her go. He thought: she wasn't watching with headphones in. I was looking at her for forty-five minutes and she had no headphones. [Artur: why headphones? Where is the connection? Explain or remove]

He went back to the hose.

---

She was back the next morning.

He saw her from the dock office window, standing at the departure rail in the same jacket. He took his coffee outside.

"Same answer," he said.

"I brought cash."

He looked at her. "How much?"

She told him.

"Saturday," he said. "After my last tour."

---

## II.

He took her south along the river as the sky went orange, cutting the engine at the bend and letting them drift.

"You wanted a tour," he said. "This is the building I'd have given you." [Artur: I don't like "I'd have given you" - reword. This is the building he want to show her, say it plainly]

150 North Riverside rose ahead of them - or rather, it didn't rise so much as *float*, the tower widening as it climbed, enormous above a base that from the water looked impossibly, almost comically narrow.

"It shouldn't work," he said. "That's the thing about it. The site was considered unbuildable for eighty years. Active railroad tracks, a city easement, the Riverwalk setback requirement - after you accounted for everything, the buildable footprint was thirty-nine feet wide." He held up his hands to show the distance. "That's not a skyscraper foundation. That's a hallway. Seven hundred and fifty feet of building on thirty-nine feet of ground."

She was looking at the building. Not at the water, for once.

"So how does it stand?"

He was pleased she'd asked. "Sixteen concrete caissons drilled a hundred and ten feet into the bedrock. And the foundation is intentionally over-engineered - it could support a building twice as tall. But that's not the interesting part."

He waited a beat. Old tour habit.

"At the very top, there are twelve tanks. A hundred and sixty thousand gallons of water, total. When the wind pushes the building - and at seven hundred feet, the wind always pushes - the water sloshes in the opposite direction. A counterweight. It's the first time that system was ever used on a building with this kind of core." He looked at her. "The building doesn't fight the wind. It moves with it. Just enough to stay upright."

She was quiet for a moment. Then: "You miss it."

"What?"

"Architecture." She said it simply. "You miss it."

He looked at her. Then he started the engine.

---

On the ride back he talked about the neighborhood where he'd grown up. He wasn't sure why - he didn't usually talk about himself. But this wasn't really a tour.

"Albany Park," he said. "Northwest side. Devon Avenue." He described it the way you describe a place you left by choice - with a kind of wry tenderness, the tenderness that costs something. The Lebanese meat market his mother still used even though there was a Whole Foods eight blocks away now. The mosques. The grocery on Kedzie that stocked the right dried limes, the specific variety his mother needed and couldn't find anywhere else.

"You don't live there anymore," she said.

"Ukrainian Village. Three years."

"Why Ukrainian Village?"

He thought about it. "Wanted to try on a different version of myself." A pause. "It mostly worked."

She looked out at the water. "Are you religious?"

He thought about it. "Sometimes."

She smiled - just slightly. He noticed he was watching for it.

Then she said: "My brother wanted to be a priest." It came out unprompted, and she seemed mildly surprised by herself for saying it. "Not the idea of it - the real thing. He had the vocation. Whatever that means."

"It means it wasn't a choice," Omar said. "It just was."

"Yeah. Exactly." She looked at the water. "He died. Car crash. He was twenty-two."

Omar didn't say he was sorry. He'd learned early that *I'm sorry* was mostly for the person saying it. He let it sit.

After a while she said: "I'm in medical school. Northwestern."

"Is that because of him?"

She turned her cup in her hands. "In a way."

She didn't elaborate. He didn't push. The boat moved. The city slid past.

---

## III.

A few days later she texted him. *Can I come to yours?* He gave her the address before he'd thought it through.

His apartment was second floor of a greystone on Damen - Ukrainian Village, proper. High ceilings, bare walls, a drafting table he still sometimes told people he didn't use. He did use it. Two or three nights a week, after the gym or after a late tour, he'd sit down and draw. Buildings on difficult sites. Problems nobody had paid him to solve. The drawings accumulated in notebooks in the table's single drawer.

Above the table, pinned to the wall: his second-year studio work from UIC. A rendering of a mixed-use residential building on a North Branch site, seven stories, a landscaped setback. The first real thing he'd have built, if he'd stayed. He'd been twenty-two when he drew it. He'd left at twenty-three, when his mother's situation became impossible to manage from a distance and impossible to explain to a financial aid office.

The drawings were still up. He'd never taken them down.

She walked around slowly, shoes off, taking the apartment in the way people take in a place they've been curious about. She stopped at the photograph on the shelf - his mother, Baghdad, late nineties, squinting into bright sun, twenty years younger and standing in front of a building that no longer existed.

Nora picked it up. Looked at it for a long moment. Didn't ask.

He made tea. Cardamom, the way his mother had taught him - pods in with the leaves, let it brew until it was nearly too strong. An Iraqi habit. In Ukrainian Village it felt like a small declaration, making it.

She sat cross-legged on his couch, shoes off, and the red hair was loose now - she'd had it tied back every time he'd seen her, and loose it was different, longer, catching the lamplight. She was slender and still, comfortable in her body in a way that some people are and some aren't. Pale skin - the kind that shows everything. When she laughed at something he said, a genuine laugh instead of the polite ones, a flush rose at her throat.

He noticed and looked away. He was not good at concealing things.

They talked for two hours. She asked about the drawings and he told her more than he'd meant to - about the UIC building, the North Branch site, the specific structural problem he'd been trying to solve with the setback. She listened carefully, the way someone listens when they're actually following the logic.

At some point he said her hair was beautiful. Not as a line - just a true thing that came out of his mouth before he'd decided to say it.

She laughed and waved it off. "Oh, the Irish thing."

"What does that mean?"

"Red hair, pale skin, freckles in summer - very on the nose, you know? Like I got the full package." She said it lightly, self-mocking. "I look exactly like what I am."

"Is that bad?"

"It's very boring," she said. "Or very authentic, depending on your take." She picked up her cup. "I haven't decided."

He didn't pursue it. He filed it somewhere.

He noticed she never spoke about herself in the future tense.

At some point the talking stopped.

He reached over and dimmed the lamp. The city outside the window. Her hair dark red against the white of the pillow. The particular smoothness of her shoulder, her arm. He was careful, almost tentative.

She was not.

---

## IV.

Gray morning light. The other side of the bed was cold - she had been gone for a while.

He picked up his phone and texted the number she'd used. *Hey. You left.* Delivered. One gray tick on WhatsApp. It never turned to two.

He tried again the next morning. Same.

He sat at the drafting table with his coffee and told himself he wasn't going to do what he was about to do, and then he did it.

Her last name - she'd given it at the tour desk that second morning, when she'd come back. He'd tried not to hold onto it. He'd held onto it.

*Nora Callery Northwestern.*

Third result. Northwestern University Feinberg School of Medicine. A small formal photograph - her, unmistakably, the red hair pulled back the way she wore it everywhere except his apartment. Third-year medical student.

He closed the laptop. Opened it again.

---

Feinberg was in Streeterville, ten minutes by bike. He went on a Tuesday between his morning and afternoon tours, still in his guide jacket - olive green, the architecture foundation logo on the chest. He was aware he looked like a man who worked on boats, which was fair.

He found her in a corridor outside a lecture hall. Scrubs, stethoscope around her neck, mid-sentence with another student. Her eyes crossed the corridor and found him and something moved through her face - alarm first, then something underneath the alarm that she closed down quickly.

She said something to the other student and came toward him fast, voice low.

"What are you doing here."

"You weren't answering."

"I'm at work, Omar."

"I know."

She looked at him for a moment. Her cheeks were red. She turned and he followed her.

---

The hospital cafeteria was fluorescent and loud and smelled like coffee and something institutional underneath. She got a cup and wrapped both hands around it. Still in her scrubs. She looked younger here than on the river, and more tired.

She talked about her brother. More than she had on the boat - the fuller version, the one with actual weight. Not just the fact of the death but the shape of what it left. The way the family had rearranged itself around the absence. Medical school had started two years after, in a burst of something she still couldn't fully name.

Omar listened. When she stopped, he said: "I'm glad you found it."

She looked at him.

"Medicine. Helping people." He turned his own cup. "I didn't. I had to leave school - my mom's situation got bad, money, everything at once. I was going to build things." He paused. "I was pretty good at it, actually."

"Do you still draw?"

He almost said no. "Yeah. At night sometimes. The drafting table in my apartment - it looks like I don't use it."

"I know," she said. "I looked."

He looked at her. She held his gaze, then leaned across the table and kissed him - brief, not tentative, unhurried. Not like the apartment. That had been dark and private and had happened partly because of momentum. This was fluorescent light and paper cups and her in scrubs with people around them. She chose it.

She sat back and looked at her coffee.

Neither of them said anything for a while.

"Meet me at the river," she said finally. "Saturday. After your last tour."

"Okay."

---

## V.

Saturday. He finished his last tour at four-thirty, motored back to the dock, and she was already there at the end of the pier. A rolling duffel beside her.

He cut the engine and looked at the bag. Then at her.

"An old roommate left it," she said. "Stuff she doesn't want anymore. I just need to get rid of it." She didn't finish the sentence. Let it trail off where an explanation would have been.

He should have said no. He almost did. She didn't touch his arm, didn't persuade him with anything except standing there, the bag between them, the river going dark in the late light behind her.

He thought about the cafeteria. Her hands around the cup.

"Get on the boat," he said.

He took her out to a stretch near Lake Street - industrial bank, low foot traffic, no cameras he knew of. She unzipped the bag a few inches to let the air out, then zipped it closed. He helped her with the weight of it.

It went in with a flat, heavy sound. Barely any splash. The river swallowed it.

She exhaled - long, slow. He kept his eyes on the water.

He didn't ask what was in the bag. She didn't tell him.

He started the engine and took them back.

---

In the days after, she asked for things. Small at first. Could he check which sections of the Riverwalk were covered by cameras? She was asking for a friend, she said. He looked it up. Then: could he hold a package for a few days, she'd come pick it up? He held it, didn't open it. Then: a man in a dark jacket might come asking around the docks - could Omar say he hadn't seen her in a couple of weeks? He said yes.

He said yes to all of it.

He was in love with her. Or something that functioned like love - he hadn't had enough examples to be certain. He went to sleep thinking about her and woke the same way, and in between he took people up and down the river and told them about buildings and looked at the water and did not examine too carefully what he'd put in it.

---

It happened on a Tuesday. He arrived at the Riverwalk to meet her and she was already there, back turned, on her phone. Speaking low, with the clipped tone of someone ending an argument rather than starting one. She said a name: *Danny.*

She hung up and turned and saw him standing there.

"Who was that?" he said.

"Someone from home."

"A friend?"

The pause was its own answer. She held his gaze - she didn't look away, which was almost worse. "He's someone I've known a long time. We grew up together." A beat. "He's my boyfriend."

The river went past. Somewhere behind him a tour boat was running - he could hear a guide's voice carrying across the water, describing a building he knew by heart.

"How long," he said.

"Since we were sixteen."

He nodded once. He didn't raise his voice. He was not that kind of man, and besides - what was there to say? He'd put a bag in the river for her. He'd followed her to a hospital. He'd told her things in a cafeteria he hadn't told anyone. He'd assumed. That was on him.

"Are you alright?" he said instead. Just that.

She said yes, reflexively, and then didn't say anything. He looked at her. She looked away. He left it.

He left.

He came back two days later. He called her and she answered on the first ring. He told himself this meant something, knowing it might mean nothing. He came back because what he'd done for her didn't un-happen because he was hurt. He came back because he was already inside whatever this was. And because he was still the man who'd watched her lean across a table in a hospital cafeteria and choose him in fluorescent light, and he could not find the switch that turned that off.

---

## VI.

Days after the bag, he was sitting on the boat between tours when the image came back. The way she'd unzipped it slightly to let the air out. The fabric he'd glimpsed inside. Green. He'd registered it without registering it.

Scrubs.

He opened his phone.

He wasn't a detective. But he was a man who'd memorized the structural logic of fifty buildings, who read a city the way other people read faces. He pulled threads quietly. She was at Feinberg. He searched the faculty directory, then staff listings, then broader - department pages, university forums. He found what he found: a professor of anatomy, late fifties, who hadn't shown up for his classes in over a week. A note on a department forum, a colleague asking if anyone had heard from Alderman, a welfare check filed. No news article yet. Just an absence.

He looked at the photograph for a long time. Middle-aged, kind-looking. Not the face of anything.

Over the next two days he asked quiet questions around the med school - carefully, at the edges. A student he'd met briefly when he showed up unannounced. A barista in the ground-floor café who seemed to know the entire building. The rumors reached him: the professor and a student. Some kind of attachment. Nobody knew exactly what. Enough for whispers.

A news item on his phone one evening: a man reported missing, South Side. He read it between tours, sitting on the boat. He almost connected it. He did not.

He had enough. He called her.

"I need to see you." His voice came out flat. "Today."

A pause. "Omar, I'm in the middle of—"

"Today."

---

## VII.

He met her on the Riverwalk away from the docks, away from everything familiar. She arrived in her coat with her hair back, and something in his face must have told her immediately because she went still before he'd said a word.

He laid it out. One fact at a time. The scrubs in the bag. The professor. The rumors. The welfare check. His voice stayed even.

She listened without moving. The composure she'd carried through every version of herself he'd seen - at the dock, in the cafeteria, on the boat - came off. Underneath it she was frightened, exhausted, and something he hadn't expected: relieved. Like someone who'd been holding their breath for a long time and someone had finally noticed.

She told him everything.

Professor Alderman had been the family's doctor since she was a child - her brother's pediatrician, one of those South Side figures who shows up at all the important moments and becomes part of the texture of a life. When her brother died, Alderman was one of the people who showed up. He'd stayed in touch. He'd encouraged her toward medicine, helped her apply to Feinberg, written her recommendation. She'd trusted him the way you trust someone who watched you grow up, who remembered you as a child, who knew the person you were still missing.

"His feelings changed," she said. "Toward me. Over time."

"And you—"

"I let it go on longer than I should have." Her jaw was tight. "He knew things about my brother that I needed someone to know. I couldn't just push that away." She looked at the water. "It was never physical. I need you to know that. But I know how it looked."

Danny had found out. Danny had gone to the professor's apartment one night, high, out of his mind with jealousy. There had been a fight.

"Is Alderman dead," Omar said.

She didn't answer for a moment. Then: yes.

She'd got there afterward. Danny was already gone - she'd called him a cab, told him to go, and he'd gone because he was scared, and she'd been the one to clean the apartment and figure out what had to disappear.

"Why?" Omar said. "Why protect him?"

She looked at him. "I genuinely don't know anymore."

He stood there for a long time. The river moved. The city went about its business.

"Did you use me," he said. Not a question.

She didn't say no. She didn't say yes. She said his name - *Omar* - in a way she hadn't said it before. Just that. The way you say something when the right words don't exist and the wrong ones are all that's left.

He stood there a moment longer. Then he walked away.

---

## VIII.

He waited a day. He ran his tours. He said the words about the buildings. He watched the water.

He was very angry now, in the specific way that comes after grief has finished - clean, cold, purposeful.

He'd already searched Danny Moran's name after she'd first mentioned him on the Riverwalk. South Side, Bridgeport. It hadn't taken long.

The bar was exactly what he'd expected - a Bridgeport Irish place with a Blackhawks jersey nailed above the bottles and a jukebox nobody was using. He walked in on a Thursday evening not entirely sure what he intended. He told himself he just wanted to see the man.

Danny was at the bar. Bigger than Omar had imagined - wide through the shoulders, thick neck, the gone-to-seed build of someone who'd been athletic once and stopped. He had the slow, careful stillness of someone managing a chemical equation inside himself. His eyes found Omar's almost immediately. He knew who he was.

They didn't speak at the bar. Danny finished his drink, left money on the counter, walked toward the door. Omar followed him out.

On the sidewalk, in the cold, Danny turned around.

"You shouldn't be here," he said.

"I know what you did," Omar said.

Danny swung first. He was bigger and probably stronger and he was also slow and on something. Omar slipped it without thinking - his body remembered what his mind had mostly set aside. The counter came from the shoulder, short and compact, the hook Reyes had spent a year on.

It landed clean on the side of Danny's jaw.

Danny went down sideways. Wrong. His head caught the concrete edge of the pub's front step.

A sound.

Omar stood there.

He didn't run. He stood for what felt like a very long time. Then he crouched down and he knew immediately - the angle of the neck, the absence of anything in the eyes. He knew what he was looking at.

He walked to the corner. He called her.

"Go," he said. "Right now. Take what you need. Don't tell me where."

Silence on the line. Then: "Omar —"

"Go."

He hung up.

---

## IX.

He went to the river first.

He locked the boat and walked the Riverwalk to the spot near the Michigan Avenue Bridge and stood there for a long time. The city was lit on both banks. He could name every building without looking — their heights, their architects, their structural secrets. 150 North Riverside floating above its impossible base. Marina City, the twin cylinders rising from the water, which someone had once told him looked like corncobs and he'd laughed because they looked nothing like corncobs and everything like what the future was supposed to look like. The Wrigley Building white against the dark.

He had given this tour hundreds of times. He would not give it again.

He said goodbye to the city the way you say goodbye to something that was never quite yours.

---

He took the Blue Line northwest, got off at Kimball, walked the familiar blocks. After midnight. His mother's lights were on. They were always on.

She opened the door and looked at his face for one second and understood. She had survived things that taught you to read catastrophe in a face.

She let him in and made tea — cardamom, the same as always. They sat at the kitchen table.

"I have to go away for a while," he said. "I don't know how long."

She was quiet. Then: "Habibi." Just that, first. The way she'd said it his whole life — when he was seven and sick with something that kept him home for a week, when he was fifteen and furious at everything, when he graduated high school and cried in the parking lot because his father wasn't there to see it. *Habibi.* I see you. I'm still here.

"A man came," she said, in the half-English that was their private language. "Two days ago. Police. He asked where you are. I said I don't know." She paused. "I don't lie. I didn't know."

He put his hand over hers.

She tried to find the English. It didn't come easily for the things that mattered. "I come here" — she started again — "I bring you here. So you are safe. This country." Her voice didn't break. It just got smaller. "I think — inshallah, he will be safe here. He will build things." She looked at their hands on the table. "Habibi. Allah yihmeek."

He didn't tell her she hadn't wasted her prayers. He didn't tell her anything. He held her hand.

He stayed an hour. He didn't tell her what had happened. She didn't ask. He kissed her forehead at the door and didn't look back as he walked away, because if he looked back he wouldn't leave.

---

He took a cab to O'Hare. Three hundred and eighty dollars in cash, his passport, a change of clothes in a backpack. At the terminal kiosk he bought a one-way ticket to Seattle on Alaska Airlines and paid with his credit card because he had no choice. He knew it was traceable. He wasn't trying to be invisible. He was trying to buy time — a few hours, maybe a day. Enough for her to be somewhere they couldn't reach.

He found a seat at the gate and watched the planes on the tarmac. He thought about engineering — the specific absurdity of something that heavy leaving the ground. He boarded.

---

## X.

The flight was four hours. He didn't sleep. He watched the country through the window: the flat dark of the midwest, then the mountains sudden and enormous, then the coast.

SeaTac was large and bright and nearly empty at that hour. He walked off the jetway and through the connector and into the main terminal.

And stopped.

Above him, suspended from the ceiling of the central atrium, hung a full-scale aircraft — hanging there in all that open space as if it had simply forgotten to fall. Boeing country. The home of the thing that made flight possible, hanging from cables above a terrazzo floor, enormous and improbable and absolutely still.

He stood there and looked at it for a long time.

He was very tired.

He thought about 150 North Riverside — the 160,000 gallons of water at the top, sloshing in the dark, keeping the building upright by yielding to what pushed against it. He thought about structures that shouldn't work but do. About sites considered unbuildable for eighty years. He thought about a woman in a hospital cafeteria leaning across a table in the fluorescent light.

He became aware, slowly, of two men at the edge of his vision. Not in uniform. Very still in the way of people who are waiting for something and have been for a while.

He didn't run.

He turned toward them.

---

## XI.

He did not contest the material facts.

His lawyer argued self-defense — Danny had swung first, there was a witness who half-confirmed it, the prior record helped. The prosecution argued recklessness: he had sought Danny out knowing what Danny was. The jury deliberated two days. Involuntary manslaughter. Four years.

He was processed and transported downstate and he did not cause problems. He was quiet. He asked for notebooks and was given them. He drew — buildings on impossible sites, structures solving problems nobody had paid him to solve, towers rising from thirty-nine-foot bases into whatever weather he imagined above them. The notebooks accumulated.

Three weeks in, on a Thursday afternoon, he was called to the phone.

He picked up. A slight delay on the line. International.

She said his name. "Omar." He sat down.

Then everything at once — not the Nora he'd known on the boat, not the Nora at the dock or the cafeteria or anywhere: "I'm alright — I'm safe — I wanted you to know that first, I wanted to call sooner but I couldn't find a way — I'm so sorry, Omar, I'm sorry for all of it, I think about it every day, I think about you —"

He let her talk.

"Are you drawing?" she asked suddenly — the question landing sideways, in the middle of everything else, as if it had been waiting since before she'd dialed.

"Yes."

She exhaled. Three weeks of something releasing in it. "Good. That's — good." Then: "I had all these things I was going to say and now I can't — I don't know what — I just needed you to know I'm sorry, and that you weren't just — you weren't — I don't know how to —"

"Nora."

She stopped.

"I know," he said.

A silence. Long enough that he could hear wind wherever she was. Something warm in it.

"I should let you go," she said. She didn't move to hang up.

"Yeah," he said. Neither did he.

The call ended eventually. He sat for a while with the phone in his hand. Through the narrow window above the table he could see a strip of sky — pale, midwestern, winter-flat. Somewhere south of that, or east, or west, she was standing in the wind.

He got up. He went back to his cell. He opened the notebook.

He drew.

*The Chicago River runs backward. It has run backward for a hundred and twenty-five years. If you didn't know, you'd never guess — it looks like any other river, moving through the city, going somewhere. You have to know the history to understand the direction.*

*Omar knew the history.*
